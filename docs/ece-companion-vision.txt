2. The complete catalogue structure
I would keep the top-level catalogue to 7 categories. More than that makes the bot feel cluttered; fewer can hide useful features.

01 · Academics
Timetable, course structure, curriculum, academic calendar, exams, attendance information if available, credits, and academic rules.
Buttons: My Timetable · Courses · Exams & Dates · Academic Rules




02 · Courses & Learning
Course-wise syllabus, lecture notes, textbooks, video resources, previous-year papers, lab manuals, explanations, and quizzes.
Buttons: Browse Courses · Find Notes · PYQs · Explain a Topic




03 · Faculty & Research
Faculty profiles, specializations, research areas, publications, ongoing projects, laboratories, and official contact or application links.
Buttons: Find Faculty · Explore Projects · Research Areas · Labs




04 · Projects & Career
Project ideas, prerequisites, skill roadmaps, internship preparation, company research, interview practice, and resume assistance.
Buttons: Find a Project · Skill Roadmaps · Internships · Interview Prep




05 · Department & Campus
Department contacts, lab locations, facilities, office hours, official forms, administrative procedures, and campus resources.
Buttons: Contacts · Find a Lab · Facilities · Forms & Policies




06 · Notices & Updates
Department notices, deadlines, timetable changes, event announcements, seminars, workshops, and registration updates.
Buttons: Latest Notices · Upcoming Events · My Reminders




07 · AI Assistant
Ask questions across all catalogues, compare courses, find relevant professors, plan study schedules, and get explanations grounded in department resources.
Buttons: Ask a Question · Study Planner · Find Anything



These are user-facing categories, not seven separate AI systems. They should all connect to the same underlying knowledge and search system.

E. General question response
For questions that don't belong to a single category, use a concise conversational answer with optional actions.
For example:
Student: "I want to get into chip design. Where should I start?"
Bot:
If you're starting with digital IC design, I'd recommend this progression:
1. Digital logic and computer architecture fundamentals.
2. Verilog or SystemVerilog and simulation.
3. RTL design and verification.
4. Synthesis, timing analysis, and the ASIC design flow.
5. A small project, such as a FIFO, UART, or simple processor.
I can also connect this roadmap to relevant ECE courses and verified faculty research in your department.

[Build My Roadmap] [Find Faculty] [Suggest Projects]
This kind of answer can combine general educational knowledge with institute-specific information. Only the institute-specific claims need to be grounded in your departmental catalogue.

5. How to make the bot genuinely useful as a one-stop solution
A common mistake is to interpret "everything" as adding hundreds of categories. The better approach is to make the bot capable of answering across categories and completing common tasks.
I'd prioritize these five capabilities:
1. Universal search: Search courses, faculty, projects, resources, notices, and policies from one query.
2. Context retention: If a student asks about DSP and then says "find the relevant professor," the bot should understand that DSP is the context.
3. Cross-catalogue answers: Connect a course to its resources, instructor, related research, and possible projects.
4. Useful actions: Open files, share official links, navigate to a section, generate a study plan, or set a reminder with the student's permission.
5. Reliable data: Show sources, verification dates, and clear limitations for changing information such as timetables and deadlines.

7. The response template I would standardize
Every response should follow a small set of rules:
- Answer first: Don't start with a long introduction.
- Use structure: Short paragraphs, bullets, and compact data blocks.
- Cite sources: Link the official source when providing institutional information.
- Add relevant actions: Offer two to four useful next steps, not a dozen unrelated buttons.
- Retain context: Allow follow-up questions without restarting the search.
- Handle missing information honestly: Say when the information is unavailable, outdated, or unverified.
- Keep Telegram messages readable: Split long explanations into multiple messages or offer a document link when appropriate.
For implementation, I would use a consistent internal response format with fields like answer, source_links, last_verified, actions, and confidence_or_limitations, then render those fields as Telegram messages and inline keyboards. The model shouldn't be responsible for inventing the buttons or official URLs.
8. Your actual catalogue should be more than the Telegram menu
Keep these three layers separate:

Layer 1: User-facing catalogue
Seven categories, buttons, commands, and example questions. This is the discovery interface.




Layer 2: Knowledge catalogue
Structured records for courses, faculty, timetable entries, projects, notices, and resource metadata, plus indexed documents for RAG.




Layer 3: Intent and action catalogue
Defines how the bot handles timetable lookups, searches, explanations, recommendations, reminders, and follow-up questions.



These layers serve different purposes. A single question may require the bot to search multiple data sources and then choose the appropriate response template.
